\begin{abstract}
This note specifies the permanent magnet subsystem for a 1--5~kW breadboard
applied-field magnetoplasmadynamic thruster running on argon, for
\textbf{continuous} operation, and reports the analysis that backs it. It
answers three core engineering questions. Can permanent magnets generate a strong
enough field: yes, 0.631~T on-axis at the cathode tip with the magnets at their
solved steady-state operating temperature, exceeding the 0.40~T requirement floor.
Where does the heat go when the thruster runs continuously: axially backwards
out of the anode, through a full-diameter copper spreader, into an external cold
plate, which holds the magnets at 72~\degC{} and the anode at 288~\degC{} with
488 of 500~W leaving through the rear interface. And would the thruster work:
the discharge model, the magnetic nozzle and an empirical fit to 667 published
argon firings all confirm feasibility, predicting a thrust of 186~mN at 100~A.
The complete assembly has a total mass of 7.17~kg within a 114~mm diameter envelope.
Finally, an end-cap geometry trade is evaluated: the baseline build achieves
0.631~T over 31.5~mm of uniform channel, while an alternative 12~mm cap configuration
delivers 0.511~T over 40.8~mm at identical assembly mass.
\end{abstract}

\section{Design Context \& Objectives}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Context}
This engineering investigation supports a 1--5~kW class breadboard electric
propulsion system utilizing argon propellant for continuous high-$\Delta V$
spacecraft orbit-raising missions. Argon is approximately a thousand times
cheaper than xenon and carries none of the geopolitical or supply constraints
associated with noble gases used in conventional Hall-effect thrusters. However,
argon does not perform efficiently in standard Hall thrusters, which motivates
applied-field magnetoplasmadynamic (AF-MPD) operation.

An AF-MPD thruster requires a strong axial magnetic field in the discharge zone.
Most experimental systems produce this field using electromagnetic solenoids,
which introduce heavy mass penalties, external power supplies, and active liquid
cooling loops that flight spacecraft cannot accommodate. This design replaces
the solenoid with an engineered, passive Halbach cylinder array of permanent magnets.

\subsection{Continuous Operational Requirements}
The thruster is required to operate in continuous steady-state mode. During nominal
continuous burns, the electric arc deposits approximately 500~W of thermal energy
directly into the copper anode barrel. Under continuous conditions without an active
conduction path, the outer casing would need to reach approximately 415~\degC{}
merely to radiate 500~W passively to space.

Because sintered NdFeB permanent magnets (grade N48UH) suffer irreversible thermal
demagnetization above 180~\degC{} and complete Curie point destruction at 310~\degC,
passive radiative dissipation is physically impossible. Therefore, the thermal
architecture is engineered to draw heat \textbf{axially backwards} out of the
anode into the rear mounting face and hand it off to an external spacecraft cold plate.

\subsection{Performance Requirements}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Field Strength:} Axial magnetic flux density at the cathode tip
        not less than 0.40~T, with 0.70~T as the ideal design goal, evaluated
        \emph{with the magnets at their solved steady-state operating temperature}.
  \item \textbf{Field Shape:} The axial field held reasonably uniform over an
        acceleration channel length of 40 to 70~mm.
  \item \textbf{Magnet Temperature:} 100~\degC{} design target against a
        180~\degC{} material threshold under continuous operation.
  \item \textbf{Mass:} Maximum 7.5~kg for the complete thruster assembly.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Final Settled Design}
\label{sec:build}
% =====================================================================

The final configuration emerged from an extensive numerical sweep of 143
magnetostatic cases and 84 thermal FEA cases. Out of eighteen candidate configurations
satisfying all field, temperature, and mass boundaries, this configuration achieves
the highest field performance.

\subsection{Geometry}
The geometry is 2D-axisymmetric. The datum $z = 0$ is defined at the front face
of the mounting plate (the surface facing the plasma), with $+z$ directed toward
the thruster exhaust.

A 1~mm radial gap between the copper hand-off boss and the Macor magnet shelf
functions as an integrated \textbf{thermal choke}, preventing conductive heat
leakage laterally into the magnet seating face.

\subsection{Magnetisation Schedule}
The 40 NdFeB tiles are assembled into five axial rings with specialized magnetization
vectors:

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Magnetisation orientation of each ring in the Halbach cylinder.}
\label{tab:polarity}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
\textbf{Ring} & \textbf{$z$ range (mm)} & \textbf{Magnetisation Vector} & \textbf{Tile Count} \\
\midrule
1 (Rear cap)   & 0 to 16   & Radially \textbf{outward} ($+\hat{r}$)          & 8 \\
2, 3, 4 (Core) & 16 to 64  & Axial, \textbf{toward exhaust} ($+\hat{z}$)     & 24 \\

This Halbach orientation forces magnetic flux to loop internally across the bore
while cancelling stray exterior dipole fields. Reversing the end cap polarities
would reduce cathode tip field from 0.669~T to 0.072~T (a five-fold drop). To prevent
assembly errors, polarizations are marked directly on drawing AFMPDT3-003, with
unique part suffixes (\texttt{-R}, \texttt{-A}, \texttt{-F}) on the purchase schedule.

\section{Continuous Thermal Design}
\label{sec:thermal}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Heat Rejection Architecture}
Continuous 500~W plasma arc dissipation demands an active conduction path out of the
anode. Figure~\ref{fig:concepts} compares four rear interface concepts under 500~W
steady-state load.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_05_rear_concepts.png}
    \caption{Thermal interface architectures under continuous 500~W load. Uncooled
    absorptive architectures reach catastrophic temperatures ($>1700^\circ\text{C}$),
    whereas the full-diameter spreader architecture (Concept D) maintains magnet
    temperatures at 64--72~\degC.}
    \label{fig:concepts}
\end{figure}

The selected architecture (Concept D) incorporates a full-diameter 6~mm thick OFHC
copper spreader plate and a sintered Aluminium Nitride (AlN) isolation plate
($k = 180\text{ W/m}\cdot\text{K}$). This expands the heat hand-off area from the
anode footprint ($1.1 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}^2$) across the entire 114~mm rear
chassis ($1.0 \times 10^{-2}\text{ m}^2$), reducing thermal contact resistance
by nearly an order of magnitude.

\subsection{Geometry Optimization Trades}
The 2~mm anode wall and 1~mm vacuum break represent the optimal trade between
thermal dissipation and magnetic bore field:

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_08_bore_radius_trade.png}

At a 2~mm wall thickness, peak anode temperature is 288~\degC{}, leaving a 112~K
margin below the 400~\degC{} softening threshold of pure copper while maximizing
on-axis magnetic field.

\subsection{Interface Cold Plate Specification}
To guarantee magnet temperatures remain below the 100~\degC{} design target,
the spacecraft cold plate joint at $z = -18$~mm must provide a thermal contact
conductance of at least:
\begin{equation}
h_{interface} \ge 1000\text{ W}/(\text{m}^2\cdot\text{K})
\end{equation}
This is readily achieved using standard thermal interface materials (TIM) and
torqued M4 fastening hardware.

    \centering

\section{Whole System \& Discharge Modeling}
\label{sec:system}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Magnetic Nozzle Topology}
Downstream of the cathode tip, magnetic field lines diverge into space, forming
an aerodynamic-analog magnetic nozzle that accelerates and detaches the swirling plasma.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.70\textwidth]{fig_11_magnetic_nozzle.png}
    \caption{Magnetic nozzle field line divergence downstream of the cathode tip,
    guiding plume expansion into space.}
    \label{fig:nozzle}
\end{figure}

Because the central cathode is a solid 6.35~mm tungsten rod, magnetic nulls on the
centerline ($z = 8$~mm and $z = 70$~mm) reside either within solid metal or 20~mm
downstream in the plume. Within the active annular discharge channel ($r = 3.175$
to 13~mm), 98\% of the volume maintains field strengths above 0.10~T.

\subsection{Current Density and Azimuthal Swirl}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_12_current_and_swirl.png}
    \caption{Computed discharge current vectors and azimuthal Lorentz body force

At 100~A arc current, the self-induced magnetic field accounts for only 0.33\% of the

\subsection{Predicted Thrust Performance}
Thrust was predicted by calibrating empirical scaling laws against the AFMPDT
database comprising 667 published experimental argon firings. At 100~A discharge
current and 0.631~T tip field, the predicted performance is:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Best Estimate Thrust:} \textbf{186~mN} at 100~A.
  \item \textbf{Expected Range:} 93 to 226~mN (interquartile spread).
\end{itemize}

\section{End Cap Configuration Trade}
\label{sec:decision}
% =====================================================================

The final design trade involves the axial thickness of the radial end caps.
Thicker caps concentrate flux across the bore, increasing peak tip field, but shorten
the uniform axial acceleration zone:

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{End cap thickness trade at constant 7.16~kg mass and identical envelope.}
\label{tab:caps}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{End Cap Height} & \textbf{Cold Field} & \textbf{Hot Field} &
\textbf{Uniform Length ($\pm 10\%$)} & \textbf{Assembly Mass} \\
\midrule
8~mm  & 0.420~T & 0.396~T & 48.0~mm & 7.16~kg \\
\textbf{12~mm (Balanced)} & \textbf{0.542~T} & \textbf{0.511~T} & \textbf{40.8~mm} & \textbf{7.16~kg} \\
\textbf{16~mm (Baseline)} & \textbf{0.669~T} & \textbf{0.631~T} & \textbf{31.5~mm} & \textbf{7.16~kg} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The 12~mm cap variant meets all field and shape requirements simultaneously
(0.511~T over 40.8~mm), whereas the 16~mm variant maximizes field strength (0.631~T
over 31.5~mm). Both variants share identical casing, spreader, and mounting components.
